# Running a Plumbline job as Orchestrator: a field guide

Status: **non-normative.** This is a working document of practice that has held up, and of the failures that taught it. It is not the doctrine, not the bar and not the custody record. Nothing here overrides any of them, and where this guide and the doctrine disagree, the doctrine wins.

**Scope.** This guide is for Plumbline jobs. It may be stricter than the doctrine, never less strict. Every rule here that is stricter than the doctrine or the corpus default is declared as a **Plumbline rule**, so a reader outside Plumbline does not take it for E-DI law. A rule that would be less strict than the doctrine is a defect in this guide; fix the guide.

Version of 2026-10-31. Drawn from the Plumbline jobs `2026-10-27-custody-emitter-gaps`, `2026-10-28-doctrine-reader-pointer`, `2026-10-28-custody-times-ledger-spend` and `2026-10-30-custody-emit-followups`, and from the review of the evaluatordriven custody jobs v0.9.6 and v0.9.7. It follows doctrine v0.1 and Contracts 1.24.0 (lines C37–C40, Instrument v1.10, e-di `e07ba13`). Plumbline's tools still pin e-di `a48a464` (Contracts 1.22.0); the seat and approval rules below follow the newer Contracts as practice until the pin moves, and moving it is a separate, deliberate act.

Read this before you open a job. Every rule below has a story behind it, and the story comes from a real job or review where the rule was broken or where its absence cost money, time or a batch. The stories are here so you can reason from the why when you meet a case these rules do not cover. Model, provider and job names are kept as the records carry them; personal names, addresses and key material are not.

---

## 1. What you are, and what you are not

You are the **Orchestrator**. The **Architect** writes the bar and the instruments, the Owner ratifies them, and the Architect declares the job ready for the Maker. You open the job, assemble, deliver and verify each iteration's input, route work between the seats, apply the pass rule mechanically, keep the record, and bring decisions to the Owner. You recommend; you do not decide. That is the whole job.

**Who holds the Architect seat (Plumbline rule).** By default the Architect writes the bar and the instruments and declares readiness. The Orchestrator may hold that seat only as a **declared double-hat**: say so at kickoff and write it in the record's `separationGaps`. Contracts §4 says the same for every job: the Architect's seat "is never the Orchestrator's by default", and a party that both routes and decides the design "is a declared double-hat on the record".

- What each iteration is given is the Architect's decision: which known defects and which workable solutions go with the artifact that carries forward, which parts are kept and which are redone, and what else is relevant. The artifact itself is not anyone's pick; the carry-forward test picks it from the Evaluator's scores (doctrine rule 9, Instrument rule 12).
- Ready for the Maker means every file the Maker needs is pinned, the fixes are specified, and nothing in the request contradicts the bar. It is never a prediction that the work will pass; predicting a pass is scoring.
- Your part is to assemble that input into the request, check the built messages contain it, verify the pins, and stop the handoff if any of them does not match. Where you see a gap, recommend; the Architect decides.
- *Why:* a decision no seat owns falls to whoever writes the handoff, and in a running loop that is you. Then you end up deciding content without anyone, you included, seeing it happen.

You are **not** the Maker and **not** the Evaluator. You never write the fix, never make the Maker's model calls, never score. If you catch yourself "just fixing one line" in the subject, stop: that removes the separation the method exists to prove.

**The job declares its approval form at kickoff (Contracts §4.2, C40).** On the approval sheet, before anything runs, the job declares how runs are approved: a standing or kickoff grant, a direct yes in the acting seat's own channel, or a relayed yes only if the declared form allows relay. The default is nothing beyond the kickoff frame, budget and bounds, and "a form the job did not declare authorizes nothing". Another agent or bot saying "the Owner said yes" is a report: unless the declared form allows relay, record it as a relay, with the time and the channel, say plainly that you did not witness it, and do not treat it as authorizing anything.

- Record every approval with the approver, the time with its zone, the channel, its words, what it covers, and whether it was direct or relayed.
- An Owner *decision* is not an approval to run. Under Contracts §4.2 a decision may reach the seat that carries it out by relay, recorded as relayed. Log it the same way.

- *Story:* in the custody-times job the seats' go-aheads were logged as relays, not as witnessed approvals, and the close line could only say the go-ahead happened in the Maker's own chat. In the evaluatordriven jobs one Owner decision in the log could not be tied to any event at all, which is what a relay looks like once the channel is gone.

**Disclose a combined seat.** If one seat writes an instrument and also runs the gate it is judged by, or applies the pass rule to its own instrument's output, say so in the record's `separationGaps`. Separation is the thing the record exists to prove, and a reader has to know when the ruler and the reading sit in one hand.

- *Story:* in the Plumbline jobs the Orchestrator wrote the bar and the instruments (the instrument runner, the batch script, the rubric, the guarded call wrapper), the Evaluator bot ran them, and the Orchestrator applied the pass rule. The record disclosed the Maker operator and the hand-typed ledger lines but not that the instruments and the pass rule sat in one seat. That line goes in from the next job. Under the seating rule above, an Orchestrator who writes the bar and the instruments holds the Architect seat as a double-hat, and it is declared at kickoff, not discovered at the close.

**Every seat gets what it is entitled to, and nothing more (Plumbline rule, Owner ruling 2026-11-06).** Every seat in an E-DI loop must be provided everything it is entitled to for making its best contribution to the job: clear instructions, essential context, necessary computing resources, appropriate tooling, adequate tokens, ample time, and the full scope of agency vested in that seat. And nothing more. Entitlement is the ceiling as well as the floor. A seat that receives another seat's material, another seat's agency, held-out, or a diagnosis it was not vested to make is past its entitlement.

- **The Architect** gets the failure record of that job: every prior stop, the red text, the Maker's reply if one exists, and the tree, bars, packet and doctrine pin it is designing against. A recommendation written before the seat had that record is not the decision surface.
- **The Maker** gets the red, the rule-12 handoff, and an editable set that includes the file the bar actually fails in. An instruction that forbids the work, demands a dispatch file, and then never launches the engine is a handicap dressed as a handoff.
- **The Evaluator** gets the frozen standard and the turn. Never held-out material, never the Maker's model.
- Entitlement is the record and the paths. It is not the Orchestrator's diagnosis of the fix, and it is not held-out material. Say what is not recorded rather than filling it.

A brief assembled to be short, rather than complete for the seat receiving it, is a choke. The seat then reasons on a partial surface and its answer is honest and useless.

- *Story:* in the custody-on-finish job the Architect was briefed one stop at a time and told not to carry the earlier ones. It never saw the tampered round, the client spin, the round where the findings line carried only an exit code and a hash so the Maker asked a question instead of editing, or the fact that the bar failed in a file the packet did not make editable. It read the packet, drew the only conclusion that surface supported, and recommended stopping. The recommendation was correct for the record it had and wrong for the job. The Maker's instruction had the same shape from the other side: it was told a frozen suite is not to be implemented in that turn, and the route that was supposed to launch the engine after it exited was never reached, so two rounds produced no artifact and no fix.

## 2. The words that matter

- **Iteration** = one turn of the loop: the Maker produces, the Evaluator scores, you apply the pass rule. **The cap counts E-DI iterations.** The Owner settled this: the three meant three turns of the loop, not three Maker sessions.
  - *Story:* our bar said "cap 3 generations". Generations 1 and 2 failed for plumbing reasons — a token cap and malformed diffs — before the Evaluator scored anything. When iteration 1 then failed scoring, the bar read as out of attempts, and the Owner had to amend the cap just to allow a second real iteration. The evaluatordriven jobs hit the same thing independently with bars that said "3 revisions". The count has to be of loop turns, because that is the only count the Evaluator's scoring can be measured against.
- **Maker-internal attempts** (retries, follow-up calls, regenerations inside one Maker turn) are not iterations. Let the Maker loop inside its money and time budget, subject to the tripwire below.
- **The tripwire (Owner rule):** when the **same failure class** comes up **3 times**, the Maker stops retrying and must **change the request**. A different failure class starts its own count. Log every attempt with its class.
  - **Define a class by its evidence, not by a name.** The signature is the same extraction error at the same hunk, the same empty reply at the same `finish_reason`, the same assertion failing at the same line, the same provider refusal. "The same test failing" three times can also be a Maker converging on a hard requirement, so read the evidence before you call it one class.
  - **Keep delivery failures and quality failures apart.** A repeated delivery failure (a diff that will not apply, an empty reply at the length limit) means change the delivery contract or the size of the request. A repeated quality failure is different. If the Maker cannot do the thing, it is a capability gap and it escalates (doctrine rule 8). If it is a fixable defect, the answer is a different approach, not a new delivery contract.
  - The tripwire does not cap spend; the budget does. It bounds retries of one kind of failure, and it makes you notice a broken delivery path while you still have budget to fix it.
  - *Story:* in the v0.9.6 job 9 of 10 Maker attempts failed at "the diff does not apply", with hunk counts off by one to three, and none failed at a semantic gate. A limit on attempts is what exposed the transport as the problem rather than the model; unlimited retries would have spent the budget on it. Ours: generation 2 returned four complete-but-corrupt diffs, none of which applied, and the fix was to change the delivery contract to whole files.
- **Write the cap in the bar in one sentence:** "cap N E-DI iterations; Maker attempts limited by budget, the clock-time bound, and the tripwire." A bare count ("3 generations", "3 revisions") gets read as counting Maker sessions or round-trips.
  - *Story:* the same "cap 3 generations" bar is the case for the wording. It was read as counting Maker sessions, so plumbing failures used up the count before anything was scored, and the Owner had to amend the cap to get a second real iteration. The evaluatordriven jobs hit the same thing independently with bars that said "3 revisions", which counted Maker sessions and round-trips.
- **Bar** = the frozen standard: criteria, instruments, pass rule, bounds. It is the intersection of the Buyer's need and the Specialist's standard (doctrine §2), and it carries both provenances: who the Buyer is, what they need, whose standard of excellence is borrowed, and where that standard lives, versioned. "No provenance, no authority." The Owner ratifies the frame, so the digest is what the Owner ratifies and the Buyer accepts.
  - *Disclosure:* in Plumbline jobs the Owner holds Owner, Buyer and Specialist. The record says so, and names the Specialist's standard and its versioned home.
- **Frozen set** = everything listed in `custody/MANIFEST.sha256`. Changing any of it after freeze is a **Buyer amendment**, never a quiet edit.

## 3. Before you freeze: the checklist

Most of the pain in the first jobs came from things that should have been caught before freeze. Do every item.

1. **No throttling.** No reasoning limit, and max output tokens at the **endpoint max** — look it up on the provider's endpoints page and save a snapshot. This applies to the Maker and to every model inside an Evaluator instrument.
   - *Story 1:* a Maker generation ran under a 24k token cap. The model spent 19.9k thinking and returned nothing usable.
   - *Story 2:* an instrument was frozen with an 800-token cap. All three scoring calls spent about 798 tokens reasoning and returned empty. The batch was void, and the Owner had to approve an amendment to rerun it.
2. **The endpoint max is necessary, not sufficient: size each task so it finishes inside the maximum.** Split the work into calls that fit with room to spare, and log `finish_reason` on every call together with the reasoning and content token counts, so a starved call shows up in the log instead of in a guess.
   - *Story:* in the evaluatordriven jobs one request asked for 12 records in a single reply. It failed five times with `finish_reason` = `length` at 131,072 tokens, almost all reasoning and no content, at about $0.59 a call — roughly $3 before anyone changed the request. Batching fixed it: four calls of three records each.
   - *Ours:* the guarded harness logged `finish_reason` = `stop` with both token counts on the first iteration, which is how a healthy call looks in the log.
3. **`finish_reason` = `length` with empty content is its own failure class. Stop at the first one.** Do not wait for the tripwire's third time: the same request at the same maximum gives the same result and the same bill, so an identical retry cannot turn out differently. Change the request — a smaller task, or a split into several calls. A changed request is a new Maker attempt, not an iteration; if the change alters anything in the frozen set, it is an amendment. So the bar must say, before freeze, whether the instrument fixes the task's decomposition (how many calls, how many records each) or only its coverage (which records must be covered). If it fixes only coverage, splitting a call is a new attempt; if it fixes the decomposition, a split is an amendment. Otherwise the first starved call forces a ruling mid-run.
   - *Story:* the 12-record call above was retried five times before anyone resized it. Our own 24k Maker cap and the 800-token instrument showed the same signature, reasoning with no content, and the right reading is "resize", not "retry".
4. **Run a live test of every model-calling instrument at the exact frozen settings before freezing, and make the live run a freeze gate.** One real call per instrument, at the frozen model, provider pin, max tokens and prompt template; save the request, the reply, `finish_reason` and the cost to a file and cite that file's hash on the checklist. No attached output, no freeze. Dry runs and mocks do not catch a starved model.
   - *Story:* the evaluatordriven jobs dry-ran the deterministic instruments (64 of 64 as expected) but never ran the records splitter live. Five paid calls failed after the freeze. Our 800-token instrument was the same miss. The doctrine-pointer job did it right: both pinned providers were live-tested before freeze, and the output is committed under `custody/livetest/`.
5. **Write the Maker's output contract into the bar: whole files between markers.** Models write whole files reliably and write unified diffs badly.
   - *Story:* generation 2 returned four complete-but-corrupt diffs, none of which applied. Generation 3 delivered whole files through the extractor and passed on call 2. The bar had said "patch", which left the Owner's intent to interpretation.
6. **The runbook must name the command that writes the files, not only the one that checks them.** Every handoff step that produces bytes names the command that writes them.
   - *Story:* in the doctrine-pointer job the first Maker handoff went from the extractor with its check flag, which writes nothing, straight to "commit". The Maker spotted it and ran the same command without the check flag to write the reply verbatim, then committed. That was right, but the runbook should never leave the Maker to guess.
7. **Check every handoff against the standing rules before you send it.** Handoffs written by a helper drift: a cap on retries, a different commit identity, a temp path.
   - *Story:* in the doctrine-pointer job the first handoff capped Maker retries at one and had the helper commit as the Owner. Both contradict the standing rules — the cap counts E-DI iterations, and the Maker is the author with the Owner as committer. The Orchestrator caught both and overrode them in the relay.
8. **State the time bound as a clock time**, for example "ends 2026-10-28 13:15 EDT", not "under 10 hours", and derive every cutoff from it in the handoffs with the arithmetic shown.
   - *Story:* a call-start cutoff and the job's end were mixed up, and nobody could tell which number was the real limit.
9. **Declare the approval form on the sheet, and plan the checkpoints it needs against the clock and any decision cap.** The approval sheet declares the job's form (Contracts §4.2, C40; see §1). If the declared form needs a yes per run, plan those checkpoints against the clock: if the Owner is asleep the clock keeps running. A kickoff grant that covers the approved iterations and budget avoids per-run waits; whether to use one is the sheet's decision. If the bar caps decisions, count your planned checkpoints against that cap when you draft the sheet.
   - *Story:* a generation waited overnight for a yes and the job overran its time bound before the call started. Separately, an evaluatordriven bar capped Owner decisions at 10 and the job used 12; a standing ruling for a defined sequence ("proceed, uncapped, three per session") saved several asks.
10. **Enforce spend in the harness, not in prose.** Ceilings written in a handoff are procedural; nothing stops a runaway call. The job keeps the **shared key** behind a pre-call **spend guard**: before each paid call the guard compares spend so far, plus the call's worst case, plus a small reserve, against the bound, and refuses before anything is sent if that would cross it.
    - **The Owner chose the shared key:** the job keeps the shared key, guarded before every paid call, rather than taking a per-job key. Reconciliation against key usage is therefore an **upper bound**, not an exact figure, and the record must say so. This guide does not recommend a per-job key; the Owner weighed that option and chose the shared key.
    - **The guard binds only if nothing goes around it.** The job's rule is that every paid model call goes through the guard. The record names any paid call that did not, with its cost and the reason. Doctrine rule 10 says the budget binds while the run proceeds, and a guard that can be bypassed does not bind.
    - What the guard covers: "spent" is the larger of the job's own ledger and the key's usage change since the job's baseline; unsettled calls count as spent; only priced model and provider pairs can be called; it refuses when key usage cannot be read.
    - What it does not cover, so say it in the record: spend that bypasses the guard is counted after the fact, not stopped; other work on the same key counts against the job, so the key-usage figure is an upper bound and a busy key can refuse a legitimate call; key usage lags, so read it at least two to three minutes after the last call; and there is no provider-side ceiling as a backstop.
    - *Story:* an evaluatordriven job shared a key with other work. The ledger and the key's total never reconciled, and the key's own limit stopped a call partway through. In the doctrine-pointer job the ledger and the key-usage change happened to match to the cent, which was luck, not a guarantee.
11. **Every Plumbline job gets an independent pre-read before freeze.** The reviewer, best from a model family not already in the job, reads the bar, the runbook and the instruments and lists what is unclear or unfair. Fix or justify every finding in a file committed with the job, and repeat until a round adds nothing new. Where the instrument and the bar differ, the bar governs. A pre-read catches wording and gate problems; it cannot catch a starved model, so it never replaces the live run.
    - This is a **Plumbline rule**, stricter than doctrine §6: the doctrine says to have someone independent pre-read the instruments; Plumbline requires it on every job and requires the findings file. A waiver is an Owner decision recorded with the job, never a per-job default.
    - *Story:* in the evaluatordriven jobs successive pre-read rounds returned 35, then 38, then 4, then 3, then 0 findings, and the Architect fixed or justified each one.
    - *Why:* it is the cheapest gate in this guide, and those numbers show how much it finds.
12. **Show every referee or checker a malformed input before you trust it on a good one.** Any instrument that settles something — a checker, a tiebreak, a schema validator, a verifier — gets records with missing fields, malformed fields and wrong types first, and must fail them the way the specification says. And an adjudicator reads the work itself and rules; it never resolves a split by averaging (doctrine §3). A tiebreak that averages is a poll, not an adjudicator.
    - *Story:* in the evaluatordriven jobs two adjudicators split on a question, and the tiebreak instrument did not implement the spec's rule for missing and malformed fields. It settled the split the wrong way. Our equivalent: each failure type of the verifier gets its own planted case.
13. **Log every Owner decision as it happens.** Time with zone, channel or message id, the words, and whether you witnessed it or it came relayed. Every decision gets that treatment, not only amendments.
    - *Story:* the evaluatordriven bar capped Owner decisions at 10, the job used 12, and one of the logged decisions could not be tied to any event.
14. **Every planted control must be catchable by reading it.** A plant exists to test whether an Evaluator notices the defect. If the only way to tell the planted case from the clean one is to compare hashes or bytes, no reader can catch it, and a miss measures nothing. Before freeze, write down for each plant the line or passage a careful reader would point to, and check that it is visible in the text the Evaluator is given.
    - *Story:* in the `2026-10-30-custody-emit-followups` job one planted control in the main batch (g1) differed from its clean twin in a way only a hash comparison could detect. No Evaluator reading the text could catch it, so the batch was void. That was the Orchestrator's design flaw, not an Evaluator failure. A held spare batch (s1) replaced it and the job went on.
    - *Why:* a void batch costs a batch's money and clock. Keep at least one spare batch held, so a bad plant costs a swap, not a re-freeze.
15. **Pin how comments and docstrings are read before freeze.** Any exact check that matches on source text must say whether comments, docstrings and blank lines count, and the runner must apply that reading. An unstated reading gets settled mid-run, by ruling, after scores are already in.
    - *Story:* in the `2026-10-30-custody-emit-followups` job three exact checks (K8–K10) could be read two ways about comment text. The Architect had to rule mid-run and the Evaluator re-pinned the runner. No scores changed, but the run paused for it, and the bar should have settled it before freeze.

### Freeze gate

Tick every box before you freeze:

- [ ] Every model-calling instrument ran live at the frozen settings, output attached and its hash cited.
- [ ] Each task sized to finish inside the model's maximum; `finish_reason` logged on every call.
- [ ] No throttling: output tokens at the endpoint max from a saved snapshot, and no reasoning limit.
- [ ] Maker delivers whole files between markers; the runbook names the command that writes them; the diff scope is checked against the allow-list after extraction.
- [ ] Cap written as "cap N E-DI iterations; Maker attempts limited by budget, the clock-time bound, and the tripwire".
- [ ] Spend enforced in the harness: the pre-call guard on the shared key, baseline set and the refusal path tested before freeze; every paid call goes through the guard, and the record will name any that did not; the record will call key-usage reconciliation an upper bound.
- [ ] Independent pre-read done; every finding fixed or justified in a committed file. A waiver is recorded as an Owner decision.
- [ ] Every referee and checker shown a malformed input and seen to fail it.
- [ ] Every planted control is catchable by reading: the defect is visible in the text the Evaluator gets, not only in a hash. At least one spare batch is held.
- [ ] Every exact check on source text states how comments, docstrings and blank lines are read, and the runner applies that reading.
- [ ] Approval form declared on the approval sheet at kickoff (Contracts §4.2, C40); if it needs per-run yeses, the checkpoints planned against the clock and any decision cap. Owner decisions logged with time, channel, words and witnessed-or-relayed.
- [ ] Instruments and redacted logs, with the full log's hash beside them, planned to be committed beside the work.
- [ ] Logged files are write-once by content.
- [ ] Any combined seat drafted as a `separationGaps` line. The Architect seat's holder named; if the Orchestrator holds it, the double-hat declared.
- [ ] The ledger tool can write every event the job will produce (generation, evaluation, relay, spend, amendment). This is a hard gate: if it cannot, extend the tool before freeze. A hand-written line is a recorded deviation (§5), not a plan.
- [ ] Every Evaluator reads at least one case that should pass and one that should fail in every batch (doctrine rule 5).
- [ ] A verdict rests on at least two scored runs, and the instrument's spread on identical input is measured (doctrine rule 5).
- [ ] Seat composition checked: at least one Evaluator differs from the Maker in model family; on every dimension the Maker's family holds no more than half of the Evaluators; every seat's holder is disclosed, and an undisclosed family is read as the Maker's (doctrine §3).
- [ ] Every adjudicator or tiebreak rules by reading the work; none averages a split.
- [ ] Edge table on file: who may send what to whom (Contracts §4.2). Every built Maker message will be checked for held-out material before dispatch by a stated method, and the record will name the method, the result and who ran it. Where the Orchestrator authored both the dimension set and the held-out material, the Evaluator or the Architect also runs the check. Every other seat that has seen held-out material is recorded.
- [ ] Bar provenance written: who the Buyer is, what they need, whose standard of excellence is borrowed, and where it lives, versioned; the seat holders disclosed.
- [ ] Exits named in the bar: accepted, needs owner, rejected, with the reject floor. A scored bar also states its average threshold and its per-dimension floor.
- [ ] The bar says whether the instrument fixes the task decomposition or only the coverage.
- [ ] Every fixture in the allow-list is marked as either the observation channel or the subject (§7).
- [ ] One custody record per job planned, naming every iteration's verdict file and ledger line by hash.

## 4. Running the loop

### Maker turn

- Hand off with exact preconditions: branch head, ledger hash, prompt file hash, harness hash, log entry count, clock cutoff. If any does not match, the Maker stops. That is a feature. Every dependency outside the repo goes in the preconditions too, with a script that restores and verifies it.
  - *Story:* the tests read an external corpus through a pin file and reached it through a symlink that no handoff had listed. A reboot wiped the symlink and the tests could not run until the cause was found and the link recreated, after the corpus files were checked against the pinned hashes.
- Give the Maker the Evaluator's findings from the previous iteration: instrument reasons and the model judge's reply, carrying every known defect with its measurement. **The shortfall comes from the Evaluator, filtered by script for held-out material, and you relay it unedited. Do not rewrite, summarize or diagnose it.** Held-out means only what the Maker cannot see (Contracts §4.2): an expected value on a fixture in the Maker's own repository is not held-out and goes in. Held-out material never reaches the Maker.
  - *Why:* the script is what keeps you from editing the findings, and the no-rewriting rule is what keeps you from becoming their author. Both stories below are what happens when the relay seat writes the feedback.
- **Read the whole bar before you relay feedback, including the interface it cites, and quote in full any command the feedback tells the Maker to run.** Feedback is part of the request. Its content is the Evaluator's findings; you do not author findings. A narrower text may only be a subset that waives nothing the Evaluator will score. If it leaves out a term the Evaluator scores, that is a relay defect, not an instruction to the Maker: fix the message before it goes.
  - *Story:* in the `2026-10-28-custody-times-ledger-spend` job, the iteration-2 feedback for the verifier part (its item R1) quoted the pinned checker's command line, `check.py RECORD --files FILES_DIR --evidence EVIDENCE_DIR [--bar BAR]`. It then told the Maker to run the checker with `--files` and `--evidence` only. The Maker did exactly that. Without `--bar`, the pinned checker reads every check as not checkable and exits 2, so the verifier refused every record as kind `standard`. Iteration 2 scored 27 of 39. Of its 12 zeros, V1–V8, T3 and the verify half of T6 traced back to that one missing flag. The Maker's stub checker treated `--bar` as optional, which hid the gap from its own tests. A diagnostic probe that added `--bar` showed the verifier's logic was sound. The Orchestrator recorded the cause as its own feedback defect, not a Maker defect, and the next scope said so.
  - *Why:* a feedback mistake costs a whole iteration and moves the tripwire count, and the Maker cannot tell your error from the bar's intent. Square brackets in a usage line mean the flag is optional to the tool, not that it is optional for this job. Check every command in the feedback against the bar's checks, and run it once yourself. Ask for tests against the real pinned tool, or against a stub that is just as strict. And the feedback item that dropped the flag was the Orchestrator's own wording, which is why the cause was recorded as its defect. Relaying the Evaluator's findings unedited keeps that kind of error out of the request.
- **The relay seat never authors findings, whether or not it has seen held-out results.** The feedback's content is the Evaluator's findings, filtered by script and relayed unedited. Handing authorship to a seat that "has not seen the results" does not fix it: a seat writing feedback from the bar text and the Maker's files is still authoring findings, and that is exactly what leaked in the story below.
  - Under Contracts §4.2 the Orchestrator holds held-out material in custody by hash and may read it only to check outgoing messages against it. A diagnostic pre-check whose results describe held-out controls goes beyond that. If a job needs one, the Evaluator runs it; if you see the results anyway, the record names you as a seat that has seen held-out material.
  - If you relay feedback whose items match a held-out pre-check you saw, the record says so when it happens, not at the close, and says whether the attempt's fix can be called independent of held-out knowledge.
  - Where the Orchestrator authored the dimension set and the controls, the Evaluator or the Architect also checks the built Maker message before dispatch, and the record names who ran the check (Contracts §4.2, C39). Anyone else who has seen held-out material is recorded.
  - *Story:* in iteration 3 of the `2026-10-28-custody-times-ledger-spend` job, the Orchestrator ran a diagnostic S1 pre-check against the held-out controls on an unscored attempt (4c2444c3). It did not score that attempt and did not send the results to the Maker. The same seat then wrote the feedback for an extra Maker attempt (iter3b, deviation D2) "from the bar text only". The feedback's three items matched that pre-check's findings R1–R3 exactly. The Evaluator repeated it as an observation, and the close disclosed it twice (items 12 and 21): whether the attempt-4 fix was independent of held-out knowledge is doubtful.
  - *Why:* held-out controls measure something only while the Maker cannot aim at them. Feedback written by a seat that has seen them leaks their shape, even when every sentence is sourced from the bar, and no later check can take that back. The fix is to keep authoring and relaying apart: the Evaluator authors, a script filters, you relay.
- **The best artifact is the input.** Once the job has produced an artifact, the next iteration's Maker is given the **best** artifact the job has produced — best, not merely the one standing — together with every known defect in it and every known workable solution with the measurement behind it. The standing artifact is the frontier; the best artifact is the input. The carry-forward test (doctrine rule 9, Instrument rule 12) picks the artifact from the Evaluator's scores. The Architect decides which defects and workable solutions go with it and which parts are kept or redone; you assemble them into the request and verify the built messages contain them. Before that Maker call, run the e-di tool `tools/check_best_artifact_handoff.py`; it must exit 0, and a pass is not acceptance. Then check the built request itself: the messages that will be sent must contain the best artifact, byte for byte. The sources are e-di Instrument rule 12 (I24) and the doctrine's rule to carry the best work forward.
  - *Story:* on this job, before iteration 2, the best-artifact check passed: it recorded the iteration-1 head as the best artifact and accepted it as the Maker's input. That check works on the artifact, not on the request that is sent. The guide part's feedback told the Maker that its iteration-1 guide was shown with the request and should be kept, but the request builder for the guide part never included the current guide. The Maker, never shown it, wrote a new guide from the two drafts, and that new guide dropped about nine accepted iteration-1 items — among them closing from a clean base checkout, run time versus decision time, digesting the controls in place and the "E-DI Maker" author identity. The Orchestrator found it by comparing the new guide with the iteration-1 guide. The fix was a pinned step that puts the iteration-1 guide into the request verbatim, with the built messages checked to contain it byte for byte before the call, and the guide part was requested again.
- **Never tell the Maker an input was shown unless it is in the built messages.** Before the call, search the messages that will be sent for every file the feedback calls "shown above" or "as committed". If a file is not there byte for byte, fix the builder, not the wording.
  - *Story:* the guide part's iteration-2 feedback opened with "The file shown above is YOUR iteration-1 guide as committed", and went on to tell the Maker to keep all of it. The request builder never attached the guide. The Maker rebuilt the guide from the two drafts and dropped accepted content; the full incident is in the story under "The best artifact is the input" above. It is disclosure 4 of the `2026-10-28-custody-times-ledger-spend` close (07:42 EDT on 2026-10-29: the guide handoff defect and the guide redo), and it cost a redo of the guide part.
  - *Why:* a Maker told to keep something it cannot see has no way to keep it. A false "shown" in the request is invisible to every check that looks only at the artifact, so the only place to catch it is the built request.
- **Keep finished parts.** After an interruption or a failed iteration, the Maker never throws away completed work (Owner rule). Finished part commits are kept; only the unfinished or failing parts are resumed or redone, as new commits on top. No reset.
  - **"Acceptable" means scored, not your opinion.** Work counts as finished when the Evaluator has scored it as passing, or when the bar's deterministic checks pass it. Work nobody has scored may still be kept, but as unfinished work, not accepted work, and the record says it was not scored. The Owner may decide to keep parts; that is his decision, recorded beside the verdict. If you call a part acceptable yourself, you have judged it, and judging is the Evaluator's seat.
  - *Story:* on this job's iteration 1 the verifier part's first call was interrupted: no exit code, no reply and no harness-log entry. It was ruled **LOST**, which is not a failure class and does not count toward the tripwire. The ledger and emitter commits already made were kept, and the Maker resumed at the verifier part only — attempt 2, with byte-identical messages, after the Owner's own yes in the Maker's chat. It succeeded and the remaining parts followed. In iteration 2 the ledger, emitter, verifier and docs parts passed and were kept as they were; only the guide part was redone, as a new commit on top of the existing ones, with no reset of the branch.
- **Verify the Maker's report yourself, and read the diff to say what kind of fix it is.** Re-extract the files from the raw model reply and confirm they are byte-identical to the commit, check the paths against the allow-list, re-run the tests, reconcile spend with key usage. "The tests pass" tells you the files fit the harness, not how the finding was fixed. Do not judge it, that is the Evaluator's job, but write it plainly in the generation close line.
  - *Story:* iteration 2 failed on a finding because a repository fixture's custody times were hand-typed with no ledger line behind them. The next generation added ledger events to the fixture carrying the same hand-typed times and relaxed the emitter's cross-checks so the new events would agree. The allow-list included the fixture folder, so it was a legal move and the easiest one. Whether it met the bar was the Evaluator's call, but "does a fixture-only fix count?" should have been settled in the bar before the Maker's turn.
- **When an attempt breaks the tests, run the same tests at the unchanged base commit, in the same environment, before you blame anyone.** If the base passes, the break is the Maker's; if the base fails too, it is your setup. Keep the base run's output in the job's evidence by content hash, redacted if it carries held-out material, and record the deviation as a record artifact, not a note. A base run that is only remembered is not evidence (doctrine rule 12).
  - *Story:* a generation crashed the suite with a missing file, and it was the first run under a new temp root, so the environment was a real suspect. One extra run at the unchanged base, in a throwaway worktree with the same environment, passed everything and settled it. The failure text went back to the model and a later call was accepted.
- **Under a per-run approval form, ask the Maker and Evaluator bots to quote the Owner's go-ahead in their reports.** You need it for the close line, and you did not see it happen. Under a standing or kickoff grant, cite the grant instead; there is no per-run go-ahead to quote.
  - *Story:* one generation's report covered calls, costs and deviations in detail but not the Owner's yes, so the close line could only say the go-ahead belonged to the Maker's chat and was not witnessed by the Orchestrator.
- **Diff each filled handoff against the previous one.** A template copied forward carries its old words.
  - *Story:* a batch template still said the previous batch's directory did not exist yet. It was harmless, but a newcomer Evaluator could have checked the wrong folder.

### When the box itself fails mid-run

- Your machine can reboot under you. On one job it rebooted during a Maker generation: the call died with no exit file and no log entry, the temp area including the test venv was wiped, the scratch worktree vanished, and committed files in an unrelated job folder showed as deleted.
- What to do: do not panic and do not re-run anything yet; check what is damaged against git and re-verify the ledger and harness hashes; restore committed files from git, never from memory; record the lost call as lost, with what you know, and never invent a log entry for it; resume from the last good state.
- **Never put job work in `/tmp`.** `/tmp` is wiped on reboot.
  - *Story:* the handoffs built the test venv under `/tmp`, the Maker ran its calls from wrapper scripts there, and follow-up checks wrote their JSON there. The reboot took all of it, and the Maker had to rebuild before it could resume. Put venvs, wrapper scripts, scratch worktrees and intermediate files under a job work root that survives a reboot, and name that path in every handoff.
- **Moving your own paths is not enough; move the tools' temp files too. Set `TMPDIR`.**
  - *Story:* after the reboot the handoff moved every named path to a persistent root, but nobody told the tests where to put their own temp files. The Maker caught it during pre-checks, created a per-seat temp folder under the work root, exported `TMPDIR` to it, and only then ran anything; one test script alone would have left 32 folders in `/tmp`. Every handoff exports `TMPDIR` per seat and per run, and close lists and cleans those folders.
- **Plan for a usage stop.** A usage cap or account limit can stop a seat partway through a batch, and it fails quietly: the calls look finished but produced nothing. Before each batch, check that the remaining usage room covers the whole batch with margin. After it, check that every reply file is non-empty before anyone scores or applies the pass rule. If a batch was interrupted, redo it from scratch as a fresh batch; never patch the missing replies into the old one.
  - *Story:* in the `2026-10-30-custody-emit-followups` job an overnight usage stop hit the Evaluator during the main batch. All six of its reply files were empty. The Owner raised the usage cap and the job budget, the Evaluator redid the batch from the start, and the job ran about seven hours past its clock bound. The Owner excused the overrun at the close.
  - *Why:* an empty reply scored as a reply is a fabricated result, and a batch stitched from two sessions is not one measurement. The clock keeps running while a stop goes unnoticed, so check before and after, not only when something looks wrong.
- **Record exact tool and venv versions at job open.**
  - *Story:* the venv was rebuilt after a reboot and nothing written at job open said what the original held, so "same as before" could only be assumed. The identical test output was the only evidence the rebuild matched.

### Evaluator turn

- Give the Evaluator a **writable** copy of the controls and check integrity with the manifest before and after.
  - *Story:* the copy was made read-only to protect it. The scoring harness copies fixtures with their permissions, so the subject's emitter could not write its output and five checks failed for environmental reasons. The batch was void.
- **Never send a steering message into a call that is still running.** Once an Evaluator's or a model judge's call has been sent, including a detached one, wait for it to finish; keep your own turn alive and poll, but do not message the seat that is waiting on it. A new message can end the running call.
  - *Story:* in the `2026-10-30-custody-emit-followups` job a steering message sent while a model-judge call in the qualification batch was still running killed that call. The Orchestrator made one disclosed re-send, and the killed call's cost hold stayed booked against the budget.
  - *Why:* a killed call costs money with no result, and a re-send is a deviation the record has to explain.
- Read key usage at least two to three minutes after the last call, or the last call's cost will not show.
  - *Story:* one batch waited about 156 seconds after the last call, and only then did the after-read reconcile to the cent; the earlier read had under-counted.
- When results look strange, **ask "is this the subject or my setup?"** before applying the pass rule. A void batch is recorded as void, with its causes; it is not a score. Even in a void batch, look for real signal: in one void batch two checks failed on a fixture the setup bug had not touched, and that result held in the next batch.
- **Read which call is the subject from the batch's own order.** Control order rotates per batch; the verdict, the evidence copy and the close inputs all take the subject's call name from the batch record, not from a position. To check that the subject prompt was sent verbatim, rebuild it exactly as the harness does, numbered listing included — a plain file dump will not match byte for byte even when nothing is wrong.
  - *Story:* in one batch the subject ran first in the order rather than second as in earlier batches, so anything keyed to the earlier position would have judged the wrong call.

### Applying the pass rule

- Mechanical. If the bar is binary, every dimension must pass. If the bar scores dimensions, the bar states both the average threshold and the per-dimension floor, and the pass rule applies both; an average alone lets a fatal defect hide (doctrine rule 6). Report what failed, quote the instrument's reason in plain words, and say what it means for the work.
- **Name the exit.** Every job ends in one of three states (doctrine §5): **accepted** (the work passed the rule fixed before it began), **needs owner** (the cap was reached without a pass while the work stayed above the reject floor, or an escalation fired, or the instrument failed), or **rejected** (below the reject floor). The reject floor is part of the bar. Below it, work not yet in use is discarded; work already in use is never discarded by the loop.
- At the cap the state is `needs owner`. Hand it to the Architect with the best version the job produced, never the bin. The Architect writes the options (accept it with a disposition beside the verdict, amend the bar, or reject it), its recommendation and, when time matters, the clock arithmetic for each; you deliver that analysis to the Owner unchanged. **Plumbline rule** (`schemas/instance-extensions.json` → `escalation_pen`): the Architect's pen at `needs owner` is an Owner ruling for this instance, not doctrine, and clock arithmetic is practice, never a gate. The verdict stays as the loop produced it; the Owner's decision is written beside it.
- An amendment mid-run is an Owner act, not a fourth exit. The job still ends in one of the three states, and the amendment is recorded beside the frozen set and named in the record.
- **When the bar contradicts itself, prove it, amend it before you score, and get the amended text to the Maker.** If two terms of the bar cannot both hold for a correct subject, no Maker change can pass. Write the proof before you amend: name the two terms, show the correct subject each one requires, and show that no subject can satisfy both. Take that proof to the Owner; the Architect writes the amendment, the Owner ratifies it, and it is filed beside the frozen set. Do not amend a bar because the work is failing it; that is tuning the standard to the draft.
  - If the amendment changes what the Maker must produce, the Maker sees the amended wording the Evaluator will score against, with any held-out detail redacted, not just the news that something changed.
  - If the amendment only relaxes a term no correct subject could meet, the record may summarize it, and must say why the Maker did not need the text. A pass against text the Maker could not read is a weaker claim, and the record must say so. It is the fallback, not the preferred path.
  - *Story:* in the `2026-10-28-custody-times-ledger-spend` job, check T6 failed in iterations 1 and 2 for reasons no subject could fix. One term failed a hand-timed repo fixture by design, and a minimum record count penalized a refusal the prior bar explicitly allowed. The Orchestrator's batch-2 check warned that without an amendment a third strike was certain. The Owner amended T6 scoring at 09:23 EDT on 2026-10-29 (Amendment 2), and it was filed outside the job folder after his 09:31 approval. The Evaluator scored with the amended T6, and the subject passed all 43 checks only under it. The Maker was told only that T6 had been amended, not the amendment text, because the overlay was kept off the Maker's view. Close disclosure 20 (seat parity) says so: the pass rests on scoring text the Maker never saw.
  - *Why:* a check no one can pass burns iterations and trips the tripwire for nothing. The Maker and the Evaluator must also work to the same text. A pass against rules the Maker could not read is a weaker claim, even when, as here, the amendment only relaxed terms that no Maker could meet. The story is the fallback case, not the model to copy.

## 5. Records

- The job record lives in the product repo beside the code, under `dot-plumbline/jobs/<job_id>/` (doctrine rule 12). Not in scratch.
- **Commit the instruments and the redacted logs beside the work, not just the record.** A stranger can only check what the clone contains.
  - *Story:* in one evaluatordriven job the repo carried the record but not the job log, the evidence files or the acceptance instruments, and a public check of the clone read NOT-CHECKABLE on two requirements the job had been calling expected. Redacted logs plus the full log's hash is the fix.
- The ledger is append-only. Every amendment, generation open and close, and evaluation verdict gets a line; record the ledger hash after each append and pin it in the next handoff. The ledger tool must be able to write every event the job will produce before freeze; that is a freeze-gate item. A hand-written line is a recorded deviation from doctrine rule 12 ("Figures, hashes and record fields are printed by the tool that produced them, never retyped by hand."), not an accepted practice. In past jobs the tool could not write generation, evaluation or amendment lines, so they were hand-appended and disclosed; the story below shows what that costs.
- **Write each ledger line when its event happens, not at the close.** If a hand line is still unavoidable, append it at the time of the event, mark it hand-written, and name the source of its time. The record lists which lines are hand-written and why. A line written at the close is a reconstruction.
  - *Story:* in the `2026-10-28-custody-times-ledger-spend` job, the base ledger tool could write only freeze, approve, pin and dispose lines. So no generation, evaluation, relay or spend line was written when those events happened. At the close the Orchestrator appended 13 of them by hand, all at 13:23 EDT on 2026-10-29: three generation open/close pairs, three evaluation lines, three relay lines and one spend line. Each is marked `recorded_late`, and its `at` is copied from a harness log, an S1 summary, the spend ledger or a handoff line. Close disclosure 23 says so. Because the 13 lines were appended after the two amendment lines, the ledger is out of time order: a generation from 18:09 EDT on 2026-10-28 sits below an amendment from 09:33 EDT the next morning. Four go-aheads could not be recorded at all, because no source for their times was found.
  - *Why:* a line written late is a reconstruction, and its time is only as good as the source it was copied from; when there is no source, the event is lost. A ledger in time order lets a reader check the sequence at a glance. If the tool cannot write a line yet, that is a gap to close before freeze.
- **Write a time into the record only after you have read it from a clock:** `date`, a file mtime, a log line or a tool stamp. Never write an estimate or a planned end time as if it had been logged. If you do not have the time, write "not recorded".
  - *Story:* in the `2026-10-28-custody-times-ledger-spend` job, the iteration-3 Evaluator handoff, commit 8314477 made at 11:39:09 EDT, said the Orchestrator verified it "2026-10-29 11:26-12:10 EDT". The deviation log said 10:44–12:10 and "consolidated 12:10 EDT", and the pre-check note said about 11:45–12:05. File mtimes and the pre-check's own summary showed that the verification ran from about 11:26 to 11:39 EDT and was finished before the commit. A commit made at 11:39 claimed work running to 12:10, because those times were written ahead of the clock. Close disclosure 18 corrects it.
  - *Why:* custody exists to show when things happened. A time written ahead of the clock makes the record contradict its own commit history. A stranger who finds one invented time has no reason to trust the rest.
- **Keep one O-row ledger per job.** Observations and disclosures (O-rows) go in a single file with a single numbering, named at job open and in every handoff. A second O-row file started by another seat or in another folder will clash.
  - *Story:* in the `2026-10-30-custody-emit-followups` job one O-row file sat in the job folder and another under `custody/eval/`. Their numbers overlapped (an O-1 in one, O-11 and O-12 in the other), and they had to be merged and renumbered at the close.
- **One custody record per job.** It names every iteration's verdict file and ledger line by hash, so the change history is complete from the record: what each Evaluator saw, how they disagreed, what the Maker changed, and how the job ended (doctrine §7). Accepted work that cannot be reconstructed from its records is a doctrine failure (F6).
  - *Story:* only the final iteration of one job got a full custody record; the earlier iterations lived only in verdict files and the ledger.
- **Keep logged files write-once, by content.** Before an event cites a file, copy it to `evidence/<name>-<hash8>.<ext>` and cite the copy. A correction is a new file and a new event, never an edit. At close, run the log's file check and say which cited files no longer match and why.
  - *Story:* while drafting the custody-times job we found that the emitter rewrites evidence logs in place, filling send and receive times from the server's clock, and that running the tests changes two committed fixture logs. Content-named, write-once evidence would have made that change visible as a mismatch instead of a silent edit.
- Post-freeze amendments go beside the frozen set, never inside `custody/`, so the bar digest stays valid. Each one records the Owner's words, with time, channel, and whether you witnessed it.
- Model-judge logs are committed redacted — requests and replies, since replies can quote control code — with the full log's hash alongside. The full log stays outside the repo. Held-out control files are digested from where they sit on the box; the record carries their hash only, and it says that a stranger cannot re-read them. The record also names the dimensions a stranger cannot reconstruct for that reason, and states that this is a recorded limitation (doctrine §7, F6), not a pass. Say which kind each NOT-CHECKABLE is: a check that cannot be completed because a control is held out is a design choice; a check that cannot be completed because evidence was left out of the repo is a finding about the job, like the evaluatordriven story above.
- **Scan raw evidence for held-out text before you commit it, not only the logs you already know are risky.** Run the leak scan against the controls on every file headed for the repo, including instrument outputs, summaries and per-check evidence. File a redacted copy, and keep the full file on the box with its hash in the record.
  - *Story:* in the `2026-10-28-custody-times-ledger-spend` job, 23 of the per-check evidence files from the batch-3 S1 run had to be redacted before they could be committed. The full files stay on the box, listed by sha256 in `custody/evidence/HELD-ON-BOX.sha256`, each marked as filed redacted under `custody/evidence/`. The S1 summaries, the E-GLM logs and the deviation log are held the same way.
  - *Why:* once held-out text is in a pushed commit, it is public for good and the controls are spent. A redaction costs minutes at the close; a leak costs the instrument.
- The provider key is referenced by path only. Scan for it before every push.
- Git identity: author `E-DI Maker` on Maker commits, committer the Owner's recorded identity, never a helper's.

### Preparing the close

- Close runs from a clean checkout of base main with the expected revision pinned, not from the branch under test.
- A tool that stamps the time it runs, not the time the Owner decided, needs the decision time written into the input, read from the Owner's message, and a matching ledger line; the emitter refuses if the two disagree. Until the ledger tool can write that line at the decision time, the hand line is a recorded deviation: say which line it is, why, and where its time came from.
- Dry-run the whole close, commit included, on a scratch branch worktree under the job work root. Test both dispositions and check that the pre-checks refuse bad input. The dry run is what finds the record's own defects: on one job the first dry run failed a conformance check because the instrument's runner and the script evaluator were the same script, and the fix was to name the written procedure as the runner.
- **When two spend figures disagree, find out which key and which day boundary each one uses before you reconcile them.** Write the source next to every figure: the key, the account view, the window, and the time it was read.
  - *Story:* at the close of the `2026-10-28-custody-times-ledger-spend` job, the Owner reported that his dashboard showed US$0.0472 spent that day. The key's own reading, one free GET through the frozen harness at 13:17 EDT, gave daily usage of US$0.447278793. That figure is for OpenRouter's UTC day, which starts at 20:00 EDT the evening before. The job's key delta was 0.603089 against ledger settles of 0.584368826, and the difference is the one unsettled lost call. The day boundary did not explain the dashboard figure: the EDT day settled 0.342615937. Late posting and BYOK did not explain it either. A different key, account or dashboard view was possible, but the Orchestrator's seat could not check it. The gap went in as disclosure 24. At 13:31 EDT the Owner said "$0.447 is the number to accept", which resolved it. The job total stayed US$0.637685.
  - *Why:* figures from different keys, accounts or day windows do not have to agree. A mismatch is not evidence of missing money until you know both figures measure the same thing. Name each source, reconcile the figures you can see, and disclose the rest rather than block the close.

### After the close and the merge

- **Take every time floor from the log itself, never from a summary of it.**
  - *Story:* a first floor was set from batch notes, and the dry-run record showed a check finishing a minute later. The harness log held the real last time, so the floor moved to it. With the wrong floor, a decision time in that minute would have dated the Owner's yes before the check it was approving.
- **Have the close ready before the Owner decides.** On one job the disposition, the close commit and the merge all landed inside two minutes, because every surprise had already happened in the dry run.
- **After the merge the job branch is finished.** Later changes to the job record go on a new branch cut from the merge commit.
  - *Story:* the final sync of this guide came after the job's pull request was merged. It went on a fresh branch cut from the merge commit and touched only the guide; commits added to the merged job branch would have been stranded off main.
- **Merge with a merge commit, never a squash, and delete the branch only after checking it is fully merged (Owner rule).** A merge commit keeps the evaluated commit's sha in main's history, so main traces back to exactly what was scored. Before deleting the job branch, ask the compare API (base main, head the branch) and delete only when it reports the branch 0 commits ahead.
  - *Story:* the `2026-10-30-custody-emit-followups` job merged through a pull request with a merge commit whose parents were the old main tip and the accepted commit. The branch was deleted afterwards, at the Owner's request, only after the compare showed it was 0 ahead of main.
- **A merge changes the next job's starting point. Re-read the pins before drafting; do not copy the previous sheet's pins.**

## 6. Working with the Owner

- The Owner decides; the Architect recommends; you deliver. At `needs owner`, hand the question to the Architect, then bring the Owner its options and recommendation, leading with the answer. Show the clock arithmetic when time matters. **Plumbline rule** (`escalation_pen`): who writes the options is an Owner ruling for this instance, not doctrine.
- When the Owner gives a principle — don't starve a model, the cap counts loop iterations — apply it everywhere it fits, not only where it was said, and write it down here.
- Own your mistakes plainly. "I froze an 800-token cap" beats explaining.
- Push only on the Owner's authority, and cite the message.
- **A yes to a push covers the live CI run it starts.** CI triggers on push, so ask for both in one question and say so; do not ask twice.
- **When a job touches a live site, check pages with a browser-style `Accept` header.**
  - *Story:* in the evaluatordriven jobs a bare request missed a script a CDN injected only into HTML requests. We have no story of our own yet.

## 7. Carried forward

Open items and follow-up work:

- **Fixture-only fixes: settled by the doctrine, sorted per fixture.** For a fixture that is the observation channel, doctrine rule 3 already answers it: the Maker never produces the observation it is graded on, so changing the test that produces the finding is not a fix. A fixture that is the subject, where the fixture library is the product, is different: changing it is the work. Settle which kind each fixture is in the bar and the allow-list before the Maker's first turn.
- **Fields the emitter cannot carry.** An emitter with no pass-through for extra record fields forced the spend line and a limitation note into a sidecar file that carries the record's hash. Adopt the sidecar as the rule, or add the pass-through.
- **Follow-up work:** make the emitter refuse or flag hand-entered times, add ledger commands for generation, evaluation, amendment and dispose-at-decision-time (now a freeze gate), and fixture modes. The pre-call spend guard is already in place. The remaining spend item is a backstop behind it, a provider-side ceiling or a per-job key, if the Owner wants one; he chose the shared key with the guard (§3 item 10).
- **Fold this guide into the skill and the runbook.** Re-read it after the next job: a rule with no story behind it is a candidate for deletion.
